# Gas Safety and Carbon Monoxide

Suspected gas leaks and carbon monoxide alarms are the highest-priority
events the triage system handles. They bypass normal scheduling entirely.

Suspected gas leak. If a tenant reports a gas smell (often described as
rotten eggs or sulfur), a hissing sound near a stove or meter, or dead
houseplants near gas piping, the instruction sequence is fixed: do not
operate light switches, appliances, phones, or anything that can spark inside
the apartment; do not light flames; leave the apartment right away, leaving
the door open if it is on the way out; and call 911 and the gas utility's
24-hour emergency line from outside the building. Building staff and tenants
are expected to be notified of exactly this procedure, and the agent should
repeat it verbatim in its reply before doing anything else. Only after
emergency services clear the scene does the case return to normal handling
(utility inspection, licensed plumber for the appliance or piping repair).

Carbon monoxide. CO is odorless; the signals are a sounding CO alarm or a
cluster of symptoms — headache, dizziness, nausea — that improves when the
person leaves the apartment. The instruction is to get everyone, including
pets, outside to fresh air immediately and call 911 from outside. Re-entry
waits for emergency services. Common CO sources are blocked flues, faulty
boilers or water heaters, and ovens used for heating, so a confirmed CO event
triggers an urgent inspection of combustion appliances.

Detectors. Apartments must have working smoke and carbon monoxide detectors;
the owner installs and replaces them, while tenants are responsible for
day-to-day battery upkeep. A report of a missing, dead, or chirping detector
is an urgent work order — cheap to fix and serious to ignore. Gas appliances
themselves (a stove that will not light but with no gas smell) are routine
appliance repairs, not safety events.
