# Leaks, Water Damage, and Mold

Water problems escalate quickly, so triage focuses on whether water is
actively flowing and what it is touching.

Active leaks. A burst pipe, water pouring through a ceiling, or any flooding
is an emergency: water spreads to wiring, neighbors below, and structural
elements within hours. Tell the tenant to shut the fixture's stop valve if
one is reachable and safe, move belongings away, and catch water in
containers. A plumber should be dispatched the same day and the manager
alerted, because units below may also be affected.

Slow or intermittent leaks. A dripping ceiling, a damp or bubbling wall, or a
recurring puddle under a sink is hazardous but not an immediate emergency.
The critical question is the source: leaks near light fixtures or outlets
raise an electrocution risk and should be treated as emergencies, with the
tenant told not to use the fixture. Otherwise schedule a plumber within a day
or two and ask the tenant for photos, the room, and whether the leak tracks
rain or a neighbor's water use — that detail routes the job to the right
vendor.

Mold. Owners of buildings with three or more apartments are required to keep
units free of indoor mold and to remediate it using safe work practices;
larger patches must be handled by licensed remediators rather than painted
over. Visible mold is a hazardous (Class B) condition. Mold almost always has
a moisture source, so a mold work order should pair remediation with finding
and fixing the leak or ventilation problem behind it. Musty smells and "the
wall is wet and smells bad" reports should be triaged as suspected mold plus
suspected hidden leak.

After any significant water event, schedule a follow-up check two to four
weeks later: paint that bubbles or a stain that returns means the source was
never fixed.
