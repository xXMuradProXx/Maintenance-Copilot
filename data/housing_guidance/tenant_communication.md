# Tenant Communication Guide

Every agent reply is also a customer-service moment and a written record, so
tone and structure both matter.

Structure of a good reply: acknowledge the problem in one sentence that shows
the message was actually read ("a slow kitchen drain is annoying — thanks for
flagging it before it clogged completely"); state the classification in plain
words, not codes; then give exactly one clear next step. One reply, one next
step: a question, an offer of appointment windows, a booking confirmation, or
a note that the manager is personally taking over.

Asking for missing information: ask at most two questions, and explain why
each is needed ("which apartment are you in, so I can send the plumber to the
right door?"). Never ask for information the tenant already gave earlier in
the conversation.

Offering appointments: present at most three windows, quote them exactly as
they appear in the calendar, and tell the tenant what to do ("reply 1 or 2,
or tell me neither works"). After a booking, confirm the vendor's company
name, the window, and what the tenant should do to prepare (clear under the
sink, secure pets, whether someone must be home).

Emergencies: safety instructions come first, before empathy statements and
before any questions. Short sentences. No scheduling talk. Confirm the
manager has been alerted.

Tone rules: plain words over jargon ("the pipe under your sink" not "the
supply line assembly"); never blame the tenant; never promise a legal
outcome, a compensation amount, or an exact repair time the calendar cannot
guarantee; and never speculate about neighbors' units. If a tenant is angry,
acknowledge the frustration once, do not argue, and move to the concrete next
step. If a tenant writes in another language, reply in that language.

Everything sent to the tenant becomes part of the case record, so a reply
should still make sense when the manager reads it three months later.
