# Hazard Classes and Repair Timeframes

Housing maintenance violations are grouped into three classes, and the class
determines how fast a condition must be corrected. The agent uses these
classes to translate a taxonomy urgency label into a concrete deadline.

Class C — immediately hazardous. Examples: no heat during heat season, no hot
water, no electricity or gas, lead paint hazards where a young child lives,
rodent infestations, and broken or missing carbon monoxide or smoke detectors
in some circumstances. Class C conditions generally must be corrected within
24 hours of notice. Heat and hot water outages in particular are expected to
be fixed immediately. If the owner does not act, the housing authority can
perform the repair itself and bill the owner through an emergency repair
program.

Class B — hazardous. Examples: leaks that are damaging surfaces, mold on
walls or ceilings, broken apartment entrance door locks, holes in floors,
defective plumbing fixtures, and roach infestations. Class B conditions are
generally expected to be corrected within about 30 days, though a responsible
manager schedules them much sooner.

Class A — non-hazardous. Examples: minor peeling paint in a unit without
young children, a small ceiling stain that is dry, a missing cover plate on a
junction box in a low-risk location. Class A conditions allow roughly 90 days
for correction.

Practical mapping for triage:

- EMERGENCY urgency in the work-order taxonomy corresponds to Class C:
  same-day action, manager notified immediately.
- URGENT corresponds to Class B: schedule a vendor within 24 to 72 hours.
- ROUTINE corresponds to Class A: schedule at the next convenient window,
  normally within one to two weeks.

Certification matters as much as the repair itself: keep a written record of
when the complaint arrived, what was done, and when, because that record is
the owner's defense if a violation is later challenged.
